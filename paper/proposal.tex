\section*{Objective}
Build a robot that performs the written \emph{secondo} part of four-hand piano repertoire on a
standard 88-key piano while a human performs the \emph{primo}, with both players at the same
keyboard. The target outcome is a working system that plays complete four-hand pieces together with a
human, evaluated by ensemble timing accuracy. Expressive timing negotiation, neural evaluation and
improvised ensemble playing are outside the present scope and are set out in
Section~\ref{sec:future}.

\section{Background}
This proposal is prompted by Wang, Zhang and Iida, who built a UR5 arm with an anthropomorphic hand
that plays chord accompaniment while a human performs the melody at the same piano, and describe it
as ``the first instance of designing and implementing a collaborative robot expressly engineered to
work with humans in the artistic pursuit of piano playing''~\cite{wang2024cooperative}. Two of their
reported limitations are structural rather than incidental: the chord vocabulary was reduced to seven
triads ``due to hardware limitations'', and the UR5 is ``not agile/fast enough'', so time budgeted
for keystrokes must be spent on lateral chord transitions~\cite{wang2024cooperative}. Both follow
from the use of a single moving arm.

We are not aware of an external key-pressing robot having performed notated four-hand repertoire with
a human at a single keyboard.\footnote{The nearest reported cases each differ in a specific respect.
WABOT-2 read a score with cameras, played a keyboard instrument with both hands and feet, and
adjusted its tempo to follow a human singer, but on its own instrument rather than a shared
keyboard~\cite{kato1987wabot2}. Disklavier-based systems play a notated part in real-time synchrony
with human musicians~\cite{muens2017,yamaha2016ensemble}, or add a responding part on the keyboard
the pianist is playing~\cite{risset1996duet,ghostkeys2025}, but in each case the actuation is
internal to the instrument. TeoTronico is an external 53-finger key-pressing robot that has appeared
in more than eighty concerts with a human pianist, but on a second piano and in alternation,
replaying MIDI~\cite{teotronico}. The system of~\cite{wang2024cooperative} does share the keyboard,
but generates improvised chords with a single hand rather than performing a written part.} This
project asks what a different mechanical structure --- a fixed array with one actuator per key ---
makes possible, and uses that platform to move toward three directions: expressive timing
negotiation (rubato), EEG-based measurement of ensemble coordination, and computer vision for score
and hand sensing.

\section{This Stage: A MIDI-Driven Four-Hand Robot}
The prerequisite for any of those directions is a machine that can hold its part in a real ensemble.
The work proposed here builds exactly that and nothing more: a robot that performs the written
\emph{secondo} of four-hand piano repertoire on a standard 88-key instrument, driven by MIDI, while a
human performs the \emph{primo} at the same keyboard.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\Alph*.}]
  \item \textbf{Fixed solenoid array.} One actuator per key over the secondo register plus a
        sustain-pedal actuator. A written secondo part requires many keys to be held simultaneously,
        which a single hand cannot do; the array also has no lateral travel, which removes the
        transition-time constraint noted above~\cite{wang2024cooperative}, and it places no moving
        structure near the human's hands. Solenoids have been reported as less expressive than
        brushless direct-drive actuation for robotic percussion~\cite{nime2020bldc}; that comparison
        concerns free-trajectory striking, and the expressive cost here will be measured rather than
        assumed.
  \item \textbf{Score following.} Real-time alignment to the human's position in the score, built on
        existing methods --- Music Plus One~\cite{raphael2010musicplusone},
        Antescofo~\cite{cont2008antescofo}, the ACCompanion~\cite{cancinochacon2023accompanion} ---
        and open symbolic-score tooling~\cite{partitura2022}. No algorithmic contribution is claimed.
  \item \textbf{Shared-keyboard note separation.} Both players' notes arrive from one instrument. Two
        methods --- keyboard split to independent MIDI channels, and subtraction of the robot's own
        issued commands --- will be implemented and compared.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
Machines have shared a keyboard with a live pianist since Risset's \emph{Duet for One
Pianist}~\cite{risset1996duet}, and a Disklavier has played a notated part in real-time synchrony
with human musicians~\cite{muens2017,yamaha2016ensemble}. Nothing above is claimed as new; it is the
platform the later stages require.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Milestones.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{M\arabic*.}]
  \item \textbf{Software only.} The score follower drives the secondo through a digital piano's own
        sound engine, with no actuators. This measures the end-to-end latency budget in the most
        favourable case, at no hardware cost.
  \item \textbf{Actuator array.} 8 $\rightarrow$ 24 $\rightarrow$ full secondo range, plus pedal,
        with a custom driver board. Per-stage latency measured on hardware.
  \item \textbf{Ensemble performance.} Complete four-hand pieces with a human partner. Tchaikovsky's
        four-hand folk-song arrangements serve as test material; Schubert's \emph{Marches militaires}
        D.733 and Brahms's \emph{Hungarian Dances} as demonstration pieces.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
\textbf{Evaluation.} The measure throughout is note-onset asynchrony between the two parts, compared against human--human
performance of the same passages recorded under the same conditions. Published reference points:
detection thresholds for asynchrony in singing ensembles fall between roughly 10 and
38\,ms~\cite{singingsync2019}; professional string quartets at 157\,bpm show onset asynchrony
standard deviations of 24 and 28\,ms~\cite{quartetsync2014}; networked piano duets are tested at
transmission latencies of 10, 20 and 40\,ms~\cite{nmpduet2021}. A secondary measure is a structured
report from the pianist co-performer --- the assessment question deferred
in~\cite{wang2024cooperative}, asked from the co-performer's side rather than the listener's.

\section{Subsequent Stages}
\label{sec:future}
\textbf{(i) Rubato.} Existing accompaniment systems assume a fixed
leader~\cite{cancinochacon2023accompanion}, whereas coordination in human piano duets varies with
texture and can involve alternating control~\cite{predictivecoding2026}.
\textbf{(ii) EEG.} Hyperscanning against the published human--human baseline for duetting
pianists~\cite{hyperscanning2021}.
\textbf{(iii) Vision and autonomous learning.} Visual score and hand sensing, and the autonomous
learning direction identified in~\cite{wang2024cooperative}, toward improvised ensemble playing.
